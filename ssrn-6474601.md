

Assessing the Benefits of
Optimized Agentic AI Systems for Asset Pricing

## Abstract
Evaluating optimized AI systems for asset pricing is difficult as analyses using his-
torical data suffer from look-ahead bias and markets are reflexive, which may erode the
patterns the AI system was trained to exploit. We introduce a real-time, out-of-sample
benchmark to sidestep both problems that measures how well AI systems explain con-
temporaneous stock returns around earnings announcements using only information
available at announcement time. Applied to state-of-the-art AI systems, we find that
the best-optimized systems more than double the explained variation in returns rel-
ative to existing literature.  Moreover, AI-based optimization provides interpretable,
human-readable economic mechanisms that explain price movements.

## 1  Introduction
Modern AI systems have quickly become more capable across a wide range of domains. This
naturally raises the question whether these models can also be used to analyze financial
markets and explain asset prices.  The apparent random movements in asset prices has
long puzzled financial economists. The challenge is perhaps most salient around earnings
releases. During a short window, stock prices display large moves which have proved difficult
to explain, e.g., earnings surprises (measured in various ways) explain up to about 5% of the
variation in returns (see, e.g., Chiang et al., 2019; Hand et al., 2022). It is therefore natural
to ask how well modern AI models can explain this variation and reduce the “dark matter”
of stock price fluctuations.
Using AI systems to explain stock price movements faces at least two challenges. First,
it is well known that large language models (LLMs) suffer from look-ahead bias as they
have been trained on vast amounts of data, including data on returns.  While attempts
have been made to mitigate the impact of look-ahead bias (e.g., Glasserman and Lin, 2023;
Engelberg et al., 2025; Merchant and Levy, 2025), look-ahead bias is unlikely to be fully
addressed unless the models are trained with hard knowledge cutoffs. A possible solution
is to train generative models with knowledge cutoffs (Drinkall et al., 2024; Levy, 2024; He
et al., 2025), but those models significantly underperform frontier AI models and can at
best provide a lower bound on the ability of optimized AI systems to explain asset prices.
Moreover, a growing literature highlights the benefits of optimizing AI systems, i.e., by
optimizing prompts, context, or updating the model’s parameters via supervised finetuning
(SFT) or reinforcement learning (RL). If the AI model has knowledge of the announcement
return, optimization is more likely to recover the information from the model and increase
look-ahead bias (Hu et al., 2024).
Second, while natural language processing has long been used by investors, modern AI
models have significantly increased the set of tools available to market participants. As a
result, in equilibrium, it is plausible that the relationship between text and returns evolves as
the model capabilities expand and investors find new ways to deploy AI. As we are initially
primarily interested in how well optimized AI systems explain current prices, conclusions
based on historical data may not carry over to the current market environment.  Under-
standing the evolution and adoption of AI systems is equally interesting, but this requires
real-time performance measurement as a key input for the reasons mentioned before.
To address these challenges, we make three contributions.  First, we introduce a new
benchmark that evaluates optimized AI systems out-of-sample (OOS) in real time to explain
stock price movements. The inputs to the model, which include the context and prompts that
## 2

the AI model uses to make its forecasts (as well as the model parameters), are determined
prior to the earnings announcement. We then feed the model the earnings call information
(i.e., the earnings numbers as well as the earnings call transcript) and let it predict the
announcement return.
Our second contribution is to consider a broad range of methods and models to assess
their performance during the last quarter of 2025 on our new benchmark. Future versions of
this paper will extend these results to subsequent earnings quarters. We provide three main
empirical findings. First, frontier large language models (LLMs) can explain up to 15% of the
variation in returns based on the earnings call transcript and earnings surprise information
alone, which is significantly higher than the prior literature that explains up to 8% of the
return variation (Hand et al., 2022). Second, this average number masks a significant amount
of inter-industry variation.  While there is little heterogeneity in the ability of earnings
surprises to explain returns across industries, optimized AI systems perform much better in
some industries than in others. We also find that no one model dominates; one model may
outperform for financial firms, while another model outperforms in utilities. Third, there are
non-trivial benefits from optimizing model prompts and context. We focus our attention on
exploring two state-of-the-art, gradient-free methods of prompt optimization: Genetic-Pareto
(GEPA) search (Agrawal et al., 2026) and Agentic Context Engineering (ACE) (Zhang et al.,
2026). Both methods follow an iterative process on a training sample to develop a prompt or
additional context that guides the model’s reasoning to predict the contemporaneous return.
We then evaluate the optimized prompts and context out-of-sample and find that it raises
the fraction of explained variation to close to 20%.
Our third contribution is to show how these optimization methods provide useful, in-
terpretable economic insights. For instance, the optimization results reveal the economic
mistakes made by unoptimized AI systems. GEPA teaches the model that “good” is not
the same as “news.” In the default prompt, the model tends to predict positive announce-
ment returns for firms with strong operating performance, record profits, revenue growth,
or buybacks.  The optimized prompt instead learns that public firms are priced relative
to expectations: a company can report strong results and still have a neutral or negative
announcement return if those results were already embedded in the stock price. The sector-
specific rules sharpen this point.  For utilities and telecommunications firms, the market
places more weight on churn and free cash flow than subscriber growth; for high-growth
technology firms, distant product roadmaps are discounted relative to immediate financial
delivery; and for financial firms, buybacks are often treated as routine capital maintenance
rather than a new positive signal. In this sense, GEPA recovers a simple but central asset-
pricing principle: announcement returns are driven by belief revisions, not by unconditional

performance.
ACE produces richer economic mechanisms because the learned rulebook captures inter-
actions among fundamentals, guidance, and the market’s prior beliefs. The rules indicate
that markets penalize cautionary forward-looking language that lowers the path of expected
cash flows and headline earnings or margins inflated by transitory items. They also show
that negative operational information can dominate otherwise positive narratives: record
margins receive little credit when they come with one-time benefits, execution failures, or
weakness in an important segment. These patterns map naturally into familiar economic
concepts, including the distinction between permanent and transitory earnings, the role of
management guidance in belief formation, and industry-specific thresholds for what counts
as value-relevant news. An implication is that optimized agentic systems generate candidate
determinants of realized returns that can be compared to the drivers of realized returns in
asset pricing models.
More broadly, our results illustrate that optimized AI systems have an important role
to play in guiding future empirical and theoretical research in asset pricing by generating
interpretable explanations of realized asset price movements—notably these explanations
hold out-of-sample.  We believe that this is a key benefit of AI compared to traditional
machine learning (ML) methods. For example, traditional statistical methods are agnostic
to the labeling of the data, i.e., predictions would be unaffected by randomizing the column
labels in the data. In contrast, AI systems benefit from pretraining on vast amounts of data
which can then be used to understand the nature of variables and how they are connected.
This pretraining serves as an economic prior and enables more rapid learning through writing
rulebooks that generalize across calls. This logic extends to many other questions in finance
and economics more broadly. To the best of our knowledge, our paper is the first to show
that this hypothesis holds out-of-sample in financial markets.
We do emphasize that, although we apply recently-developed methods to frontier LLMs
and find significant gains already, we think that we have only scratched the surface and
that further improvements are within reach. For instance, we have not used information
(i) from earlier earnings calls (e.g., some CEOs may generally be more optimistic than
others), (ii) from peers’ earnings calls or (iii) additional information that is typically available
right before a call to pinpoint what specific items analysts are looking for in the upcoming
call.  Moreover, model capabilities are expanding rapidly as are the prompt and context
optimization methods.  This makes for a clear and exciting journey to discovering what
moves asset prices. Also, the benchmark naturally refreshes itself as the topics and themes
that drive markets (e.g., from the great financial crisis to the European sovereign debt crisis
or the COVID-19 pandemic) change and this may modify the connection between text and

returns. In the ultimate case where the benchmark gets saturated, as has been the case with
many other AI benchmarks, we have made great strides in understanding how investors price
key information events.
To simplify and coordinate the effort to discover how optimized AI system can be used

researchers to improve on our results on explainingmarkets.ai.
Related literature  Our paper builds on a vast literature showing that earnings announce-
ments convey value-relevant information.  The empirical foundation begins with Beaver
(1968), who shows that accounting earnings announcements are associated with movements
in stock prices, trading volume, and investor belief revision, thereby making earnings re-
leases a natural setting for studying how markets process firm-level information. Related,
Ball and Brown (1968) show that while the market prices a large portion of the information
content in an earning announcement ex ante, the market tends to under-react on average,
i.e., the prices exhibit “post-earnings announcement drift.” Bernard and Thomas (1989) and
Bernard and Thomas (1990) extend this literature by showing that delayed price responses
are linked to the implications of current earnings for future earnings, suggesting limits to
immediate market processing even for public, salient disclosures. Kothari (2001) synthesizes
this broader capital-markets research agenda around value relevance, market efficiency, fun-
damental analysis, and the interpretation of accounting information in asset prices.  Our
paper also relates to a long literature in asset pricing, building on the seminal work of Fama
et al. (1969), to explain realized movements in stock returns. Relative to this literature, our
setting keeps the earnings-announcement event at the center but asks whether modern AI
systems can explain contemporaneous announcement returns using only information avail-
able at announcement time, rather than focusing primarily on delayed drift or on a fixed
accounting signal.
The paper also relates to research showing that qualitative and nonnumerical disclo-
sure contains information beyond headline earnings numbers. Tetlock (2007) demonstrates
that media pessimism extracted from financial news predicts market returns and trading
volume, linking textual tone to investor sentiment and price pressure. Loughran and Mc-
Donald (2011) show that dictionary-based sentiment measures must be adapted to financial
language, because generic negative-word lists misclassify common business terms and can
distort inference in financial applications.  Mayew and Venkatachalam (2012) extend the
disclosure literature beyond written text by showing that managers’ vocal affect during
earnings conference calls is informative about future firm performance and is incremental to
both quantitative earnings information and linguistic content. These papers motivate the
## 5

view that market participants respond not only to numerical earnings surprises but also to
softer information embedded in narratives, tone, language, and delivery.
Our contribution is to move from handcrafted disclosure measures to optimized agentic
AI systems that extract and combine signals from earnings-announcement information in
real time. Because frontier models may have been trained on historical outcomes, historical
backtests of AI performance in asset pricing can suffer from look-ahead bias; and because
markets are reflexive, relationships between disclosures and returns may evolve as investors
adopt new AI tools. We therefore introduce a real-time, out-of-sample benchmark for eval-
uating how well optimized AI systems explain announcement-window returns using only
contemporaneously available information, including earnings-call transcripts and announce-
ment data. Moreover, the AI systems generate interpretable, human-readable mechanisms
that can be evaluated out of sample and compared with the drivers of realized returns in
asset pricing models, thereby linking the earnings-announcement and soft-disclosure litera-
tures to a broader question of how modern AI can help empirical measurement of information
processing in financial markets.
2  Benchmarking Agentic AI Systems
Evaluating the extent to which AI systems can explain asset prices requires a benchmark
that is both methodologically rigorous and economically meaningful, e.g., free of look-ahead
bias and focused on a task that is relevant. In this section, we outline the desirable features of
such a benchmark, formally define its structure, and explain our approach to out-of-sample
evaluation.
2.1  Desirable Features of a Benchmark
The application of LLMs, agentic tooling, and AI models in a more general sense to a variety
of domains has shown rapid progress (see, e.g., Jumper et al., 2021). Moreover, a growing
literature highlights the benefits of optimizing AI systems by modifying prompts, context, or
model parameters (Ouyang et al., 2022; Khattab et al., 2024; Agrawal et al., 2026). Although
a variety of domains have seen rapid progress, it is unclear whether we should expect similar
benefits when applying these methods to tasks involving financial markets.
For example, the fact that long time-series of content are used to train these models
implies that the models will exhibit look-ahead bias for many in-sample tasks.  Layering
optimization on top of such models and systems is likely to exacerbate the bias. Even if one
ignores look-ahead bias, the reflexivity of markets implies that the benefits of the technology
## 6

may not hold out of sample, i.e., the value of information or technology today differs from
its value tomorrow if investors adjust their models and strategies in response to profitable
trading opportunities. Thus, empirical research involving AI systems needs to adapt, and
studying the adaptation of markets to new AI methods requires measurement on a real-time
out-of-sample benchmark.
Acknowledging the differences and challenges associated with empirical research involving
AI, we first outline the desirable features of any benchmark:
- Construct Validity - the benchmark measures what it claims to measure.
- Contamination Resistance - “answers” should not be readily available due to leakage
at training or inference time.
- Ecological Validity - alignment with real-world task performance.
- Discriminative Power - systems with different capabilities achieve meaningfully differ-
ent scores.
- Reproducibility & Transparency - benchmark is fully and openly specified.
Guided by these features, we design a benchmark that examines the extent to which
systems can explain the consequences of information releases on asset prices. As we will
show in the next section, when such a benchmark is evaluated in real-time, it naturally
satisfies these features.

## 2.3  A First Implementation: Earnings Announcements
Our implementation of the benchmark focuses on corporate earnings announcements (EAs)
for three primary reasons. First, a rich literature spanning more than five decades has sought
to explain the returns surrounding earnings announcements. Progress from the earliest it-
erations to today has been an increase in explanatory power from roughly 2% to 8%, with
much of this coming from more robust measurement (Chiang et al., 2019) or from incor-
porating numerous surprises along dimensions other than just earnings (Hand et al., 2022).
This baseline suggests that the benchmark will either demonstrate significant discriminative
power across AI models, or reveal that asset prices around these events are overwhelmingly
driven by unobservable noise that cannot be interpreted using current AI models.
Second, EAs are information-rich events involving content across a variety of modalities:
text, audio, and sometimes visual presentations. As such, the market reaction is likely a
function of both quantitative and qualitative information.  This multi-modal synthesis is
precisely where LLMs and AI agents are expected to add significant value beyond what
historical, lexicon-based methods have achieved.
Third, there are more than 1,000 EAs every quarter. Even assuming moderate effect
sizes, this yields a sufficiently large sample to provide statistically powerful, real-time tests
of performance differences across systems, while being highly confident that the benchmark
is robust against data contamination.

We follow a strict temporal protocol when evaluating the benchmark to ensure that pre-
dictions are t‘ruly out-of-sample and free from look-ahead bias. Specifically, a calendar of
expected earnings announcements (EAs) is maintained to facilitate the collection of a fair
ex ante information set, which is used to approximate Ω
t−
prior to an event. Once an EA

We want to highlight that this strict temporal protocol applies at the observation level.
Thus, information learned earlier in a given evaluation period can—and likely should—be
used when making predictions later in the period. Along these lines, our initial evaluations
use the first half of a quarter to optimize AI systems, which are then applied to the second half
of the quarter. We find that this leads to a significant increase in performance. Given these
results, while we choose to split the quarter evenly, there is no reason that this optimization
and learning cannot be applied online in real-time.

3.4.2  Genetic-Pareto Prompt Optimization
GEPA (Agrawal et al., 2026) is a prompt optimization algorithm that iteratively improves
the instructions given to an LLM, i.e., the prompt or context p above. The method maintains
a population of candidate prompts and evolves them through repeated selection, mutation,
and evaluation. At each iteration, GEPA selects a candidate from the Pareto frontier—the
subset of prompts that each achieve the highest score on at least one training instance—
sampling in proportion to the number of instances on which each candidate leads.  This
selection mechanism encourages diversity across the candidate pool.
Once a prompt has been selected, it is applied to a mini-batch of training examples,
and a scoring function identifies where the model succeeds and fails, returning textual diag-
nostics describing failure patterns. An LLM then reads the current prompt alongside these
diagnostics and proposes a targeted revision, i.e., “reflective mutation.” This reflection step
involves the model itself diagnosing shortcomings in natural language rather than relying
on random perturbations or gradient signals. If the revised prompt improves performance
on the mini-batch, it enters the candidate pool; otherwise it is discarded. The algorithm
terminates when a computational budget is exhausted and returns the candidate with the
highest average score on a held-out evaluation set.

3.4.3  Agentic Context Engineering (ACE)
ACE, introduced by Zhang et al. (2026), is a framework for optimizing the context provided
to an LLM by building a structured collection of rules—termed a “playbook” or “rulebook”—
through an iterative generate-reflect-curate loop.  In each iteration, a Generator module
receives the current playbook and a task instance, attempts the task while recording its rea-
soning, and flags which existing rules proved useful or misleading. A Reflector module then
analyzes this record, identifies patterns in errors and successes, and proposes targeted addi-
tions or modifications—small “delta” updates rather than a wholesale rewrite of the context.
Finally, a Curator module integrates these deltas into the playbook: new rules are appended,
existing rules are updated based on whether they improved or degraded performance, and
## 18

duplicate or overlapping rules are removed.
This incremental architecture addresses a key failure mode of monolithic prompt opti-
mization: because each iteration modifies only a small portion of the context, the method
avoids “context collapse,” in which successive LLM rewrites progressively compress and dis-
card accumulated knowledge. The result is a modular, interpretable set of rules that grows
and refines over time.

The ACE rulebook, like the GEPA prompt, is interpretable.  It contains specific, ac-
tionable heuristics that reflect how markets actually respond to different types of earnings
news (see Figure 3). For instance, the rulebook instructs the model that when a pre-profit
company reports revenue growth exceeding 30% year-over-year but operating losses remain
flat, the prediction should fall in the 0.10–0.20 range, because markets demand that high
growth translate into financial improvement. Similarly, when management uses cautionary
forward-looking language—phrases such as “aggressive consensus” or “gradual recovery”—the
rulebook directs predictions to the 0.35–0.50 range. The rulebook also identifies interaction
effects: when record margins are attributed to non-recurring benefits (such as precious met-
als revaluations) and the same quarter features an operational execution failure alongside a
major geographic segment declining by more than 15%, the prediction should be 0.10–0.20
regardless of positive demand narratives elsewhere in the transcript. These rules echo well-
established findings in the accounting and finance literature regarding the market’s differen-
tial response to transitory versus permanent earnings components, but they are discovered
entirely from data by the agentic system.

4  On the Differences between Agentic Optimization and
## Traditional Machine Learning
The agentic optimization methods that we explore provide, at least, two important benefits
over traditional machine learning (ML) methods. The first benefit relates to efficiency. Both
methods leverage contextualized feedback during optimization, i.e., after a model reasons
about a specific instance of a forecasting problem and makes a prediction, its prediction is
compared to the realized value and feedback is given on why the prediction was good or bad.
This feedback—contextualized by the specific instance at hand—allows an LLM to learn
more efficiently from even a single observation.
## 3
To see this, consider a binary classification task where traditional ML methods learn
by minimizing some loss function over predictions and targets: 0’s and 1’s (e.g., successful
and unsuccessful predictions). The only feedback in this setting is the gradient of the loss,
which for any single observation may be incredibly noisy.  In contrast, ACE and GEPA
explicitly reflect on why the prediction was (un)successful, a process which leverages the
## 3
Agrawal et al. (2026) illustrate the efficiency gains of GEPA compared to traditional reinforcement
learning methods.
## 20

existing knowledge in the LLM and any information encoded in the current version of the
rulebook or prompt.
To provide a simple analogy, a child learning math who receives only “correct” or “incor-
rect” as feedback, may take a long time to master the subject. If, instead, the child receives
additional feedback why a particular answer is correct or not, she will learn more efficiently.
In our context, an LLM reflecting on why a particular forecast is too optimistic or pessimistic
may lead to rules that generalize to other earnings calls.
AI-based optimization provides a related benefit by being able to leverage knowledge
acquired during pretraining regarding the economic meaning of features and how they are
related. In contrast, traditional ML methods are unaware of the relationship between vari-
ables and those need to be learned from data. Thus, LLMs’ basic understanding how certain
variables are related may help in generating predictions. As a simple example, if we provide
an agentic AI system information on sales and cost of goods sold, it will understand that
it also has information about profitability and margins. A machine learning algorithm does
not have this prior economic understanding and context, and will have to learn it from data.
The second key benefit is that the optimized prompts and context may identify new de-
terminants of realized returns around key news events. In particular, asset pricing models
have implications for realized returns, in addition to implications for moments of the return
distribution such as expected returns and (co)variances. If the rulebooks learned by ACE
or the prompts learned by GEPA do not have natural counterparts in current asset pricing
models, it may be worth considering such extensions. Further, the optimized context of-
ten provides rules that are specific to a certain industry, which may be helpful to develop
industry-specific models. As a concrete example, if changes in guidance are of first-order
importance to explain announcement returns, asset pricing models ideally account for guid-
ance provided by firms to investors. This fits in a rich tradition to explore models of beliefs
for investors and firm management, potentially going beyond rational expectations.
As the prompts and context are learned from data, AI-based optimization adds a valuable
tool to the toolbox of empirical asset pricing researchers by integrating empirical learning
with economic structure.
5  A New Competition to Explain Asset Prices
Our results thus far suggest that AI systems can materially increase our understanding of
asset prices around earnings announcements. While these results are a contribution on their
own, we do not intend for them to be the “definitive” results. For example, we anticipate
further progress from including historical events, incorporating peers, or improved finance-
## 21

specific harnesses for agents.
Rather than working on these in a silo, we are introducing a competition with the explicit
goal of improving our understanding of asset prices around information releases—related,
Hellum et al. (2025) introduce a broad competition to identify the pricing kernel, in contrast,
our aim is focused on understanding how information is priced.  This follows a broader
tradition in asset pricing to benchmark asset pricing models by their ability to explain
differences in average returns. However, given the volatility of returns, it is challenging to
discriminate between models during a short out-of-sample period. Our benchmark instead
can be evaluated during a single earnings season, which allows for the rapid evolution of
optimized AI systems.
## 4
In line with the principles in Section 2, the competition is open to anyone—the cost of
running Gemini in our experiments was much less than $10 for an entire earnings season—and
evaluated transparently. Participants need only register their system with the competition
on explainingmarkets.ai to receive programmatic access to (i) a common EA calendar and
(ii) an opportunity to log a prediction in real-time when an EA occurs.  By defining a
common set of events, evaluation methodology, and introducing a competition, we aim to
spur progress on our understanding of asset prices similarly to how the introduction of the
ImageNet Large Scale Visual Recognition Challenge (Deng et al., 2009) (i) led to dramatic
decreases in the error rates of supervised models over only a few years (Russakovsky et
al., 2015), and (ii) supported subsequent development of scalable self-supervised learning
methods within computer vision (see, e.g., Chen et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021).
## 6  Conclusions
Evaluating optimized AI systems for asset pricing is fundamentally difficult for at least two
reasons. First, AI models are trained on all data, implying that any backtest or analysis using
historical data suffers from look-ahead bias. In addition, markets are reflexive — as investors
adopt AI, prices adjust — which may erode the very patterns the AI system was trained
to exploit. We introduce a real-time, out-of-sample benchmark designed to sidestep both
problems. The benchmark measures how well AI systems can explain contemporaneous stock
returns around earnings announcements using only information available at announcement
time, including the announcement text itself.
